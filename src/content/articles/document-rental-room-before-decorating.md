Before you move the sofa, change a cabinet pull, or attach anything to a wall, make a small record of the area you plan to change. A close-up of a scratch is more useful when another photo shows which wall it belongs to. A bag of original screws is more useful when a note identifies the cabinet they came from.

The goal is a record you can understand later, without searching hundreds of phone photos. Start with one project area, connect each detail to a location, and keep the record with the product instructions and any permission. This guide covers optional decorating; it does not replace a required inspection, repair report, or your rental's approval process, and it cannot guarantee a deposit outcome.

## Define the area before taking photos

Write one sentence describing the proposed change: “Rearrange the reading corner and consider one lightweight picture on the wall beside the window.” Give the location a plain name such as **living room, window wall**. Use that name in your notes and filenames instead of relying on left and right, which change when you turn around.

Record the actual date. If you moved in months ago, call these photos a **pre-project record taken today**, not a move-in record. Keep earlier inspection photos separately so the dates and purposes remain clear.

Read the decorating conditions in your current agreement and confirm the required approval before doing the work. Our [rental update request guide](/articles/ask-landlord-permission-rental-update/) helps you describe a specific proposal. Taking photos is preparation, not permission.

## Take a wide view, a location view, and a detail

Use this repeatable sequence for each surface that the project will touch:

1. **Wide view:** Photograph the whole project area from a recognizable position, such as the doorway. Include the nearby window, room corner, or cabinet run that identifies it.
2. **Location view:** Move closer while keeping a fixed feature in the frame. Show where the proposed attachment or furniture position sits relative to the baseboard, window trim, or cabinet edge.
3. **Detail:** Photograph any visible mark, filled hole, finish change, or damaged area close enough to describe it. Keep the location view as its partner.

Review the images at full size before rearranging anything. If glare hides a mark, change your position or the lighting and take another photo. If a close-up is blurry, retake it. These are practical recording choices, not a professional inspection standard.

Do not put tape, a sticky note, or a new adhesive on the wall just to identify a photo. Add the location in a separate note or on a copy of the image. Keep the original photo unchanged; a labeled copy can help explain a location without replacing it.

For a U.S. consumer example, the [Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services](https://www.fdacs.gov/Consumer-Resources/Landlord-Tenant-Law-in-Florida) recommends photos or video and notes of questionable rental conditions, and keeping copies of correspondence. Its guidance concerns Florida rentals and the start of a tenancy. The project sequence here is our own organizing method, not a statement of inspection duties in every state.

## Describe what you can see, without guessing the cause

Write “gray scuff above baseboard beside window” rather than “old moving damage.” A photo and a note can show what you observed; they do not establish when a mark first appeared or who caused it. If the paint type or age is unknown, write **unknown** instead of inferring it from appearance.

Photograph the visible surface before cleaning, covering, or changing it. If you find a condition that needs management's attention, use the appropriate reporting process before proceeding with the optional update. Do not turn the documentation step into sanding, scraping, or an improvised repair.

For a hardware change, photograph each original part in place and record its location before any approved removal. Keep parts grouped by that location. Our [cabinet pull fit check](/articles/measure-cabinet-pulls-before-replacing/) explains why existing holes and the area hidden by the old base matter. A photo alone does not supply those measurements.

## Keep the product instructions beside the photos

A before photo cannot tell you that an adhesive will suit the finish. Save the exact product name, model, package directions, surface restrictions, and removal method before deciding to use it. If the product changes, update the record before installation.

For example, [Command's U.S. picture hanging strip instructions](https://www.command.com/3M/en_US/command/how-to-use/picture-hanging-strips/) exclude wallpaper and warn that failing to follow directions can cause damage. They also specify a removal direction along the wall. These are instructions for those products, not a universal method for removing every adhesive. Save the directions supplied with your particular product and retain access to any removal tabs it requires.

If the surface is uncertain, pause the attached-product plan and check with management or the manufacturer. The [removable hook wall checklist](/articles/removable-hooks-rental-wall-checklist/) provides a more detailed compatibility decision. A freestanding decorative alternative may be simpler, provided its own instructions and any required safety installation are followed.

## Example: a reading corner with one visible mark

Imagine a renter planning to move an existing chair beside the living room window and considering a small picture. This is a hypothetical planning example, not a tested project.

The renter notices a gray mark above the baseboard. They take a doorway photo showing the window wall, a closer view containing the window trim and mark, and a detail of the mark. They write “visible before this project; earlier history unknown.” They do not describe it as move-in damage because they have no earlier record showing it.

The files become `2026-10-28_living-window_01-wide.jpg`, `2026-10-28_living-window_02-location.jpg`, and `2026-10-28_living-window_03-scuff.jpg`. A short note identifies the project, photo date, and proposed product. The date in the filename is an organizing aid, not independent verification of when the image was captured.

Suppose management's reply allows rearranging the furniture but leaves the picture attachment unresolved. The renter can continue planning the chair position while keeping the wall attachment on hold. The condition record remains useful even if the picture is never installed. For the layout itself, start with [reversible living room updates](/articles/reversible-living-room-updates/) and keep the route through the room usable.

## Copy this project record

Use one row for each location or item. Keep a separate list of any questions awaiting an answer, so an incomplete record does not look like a completed check.

| Field | What to record | Reading-corner example |
|---|---|---|
| Scope and date | Proposed change and actual recording date | Chair layout; possible picture; October 28, 2026 |
| Location | Room plus fixed feature | Living room, window wall |
| Photo set | Wide, location, and detail filenames | Files 01–03 in the project folder |
| Visible condition | Plain description; unknowns stated | Gray scuff above baseboard; earlier history unknown |
| Permission | Original request, reply, and open questions | Layout allowed; attachment awaiting clarification |
| Product record | Exact model and saved directions | Candidate only; not purchased or installed |
| Original parts | Which parts, where stored, or not applicable | None removed |
| Follow-up | Completion, changed scope, and removal record | Add only when the next stage actually happens |

You do not need a paid app. Use a folder and an ordinary document or note you can export. Keep the original photos, notes, permission messages, and instructions together; [our home manual organization guide](/articles/organize-home-product-manuals/) offers a simple naming approach for product documents.

Open the saved folder from the place you intend to keep it and check that the photos and attachments are readable. Keep another copy in an existing backup location and verify it opens. Before sending selected photos to management, check the frame for mail, account numbers, faces, or other unrelated personal details. Retain your originals privately and share only what is needed through the appropriate channel.

## Add a matching record when the project changes

After an approved update, photograph the same area from similar positions and add the completion date. Record any change from the approved scope before going further. If the update is later removed, add the actual removal date, matching photos, and any follow-up message; do not overwrite the before set.

Finish with a quick retrieval check: could you find the original condition, exact product, permission, and any retained parts without relying on memory? If one is missing, resolve that gap before starting the next optional change. A small record that answers those questions is more useful than an unlabeled camera roll.
