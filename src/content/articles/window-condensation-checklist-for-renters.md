Water on the inside of a window deserves attention, but a single photograph cannot identify every cause. Start by noting where and when the moisture appears. In a rental, that record can help your property manager investigate a recurring issue.

## Identify the location of the moisture

Is the moisture on the room-facing glass, the outside, or apparently between panes? Record that distinction without dismantling the window. Also note whether the frame, sill, or surrounding wall is wet.

Water entering during rain is a different observation from droplets appearing after a shower. If you see an active leak, persistent damage, or moisture near electrical equipment, report it promptly and avoid unsafe investigation. Do not climb outside or remove glazing to improve the diagnosis.

## Keep a short observation log

For several days, record the room, time, approximate outdoor conditions, and recent activities such as cooking or showering. Photograph the affected area and include a wider view that shows its location. This is useful context, not proof that one particular activity caused the problem.

If you have a humidity meter, record its reading and location. Consumer meters vary, so use the result as an observation rather than a precise building assessment. Note whether readings and condensation change after ordinary ventilation or reduced moisture generation.

## Check everyday moisture sources

EPA guidance connects indoor moisture control with reducing mold risk and recommends keeping relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally around 30–50 percent when possible. That broad range is not a guarantee against condensation on every cold window; climate and building conditions matter.

Use existing bathroom and kitchen exhaust systems as intended. Report a fan that does not seem to work instead of altering its wiring or ducting. Where opening a window is safe and practical, ventilation may help, but outdoor conditions, security, and building rules affect that decision.

## Dry visible moisture and keep track

Wipe ordinary surface moisture with an appropriate cloth and follow the window finish's care instructions. Do not seal over damp material, paint over a recurring problem, or cover it with decoration. Repeated wetting needs investigation of the cause.

If there is visible mold, use the EPA's guidance and involve management as appropriate. This checklist is not a mold-cleanup procedure or health diagnosis. Extensive contamination, ongoing water damage, or any uncertainty about safe cleanup calls for qualified advice.

## Send a useful report to management

Describe the location, frequency, and what has changed. An example is: “The inside lower edge of the bedroom window is wet each morning. I have attached dated photos and noted humidity readings. Please check the recurring moisture and the damp sill.”

Avoid claiming that a failed seal, poor insulation, or tenant activity is definitely responsible unless it has been assessed. Ask what inspection or repair is appropriate. Keep your observations and responses together so a recurring issue does not become a disconnected series of reports.

## Know what this checklist cannot establish

An observation log cannot determine hidden moisture damage, legal responsibility, or a medical risk. Do not use a generic humidity target to dismiss visible water, and do not use a dehumidifier to conceal an unresolved leak. The practical goal is to reduce avoidable moisture while getting the underlying problem assessed.
