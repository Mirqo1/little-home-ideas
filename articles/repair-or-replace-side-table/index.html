Repair or Replace a Side Table? Compare the Whole Job

Compare a side-table repair estimate with a real replacement, including scope, transport, timing, and unresolved costs. Use the original decision worksheet.

By Little Home Ideas · Published October 30, 2026

A worn side table presents two different questions: does it still do its job, and do you want its appearance changed? A repair estimate may answer only one of them. A replacement listing may leave out delivery, assembly, and what happens to the old table.

Before deciding, compare the finished result in your room, not a repair headline against a furniture price tag. Define the job, get the missing details in writing, and keep “wait” available for a purely cosmetic change when the table is suitable for continued use. This is a planning guide, not a way to diagnose damage or perform a repair.

Separate a cosmetic wish from a condition that needs attention

Write what you can observe: “light scratches on the top,” “finish worn at the front edge,” or “the table moves when used normally.” Do not turn those notes into a diagnosis such as “just needs glue.” Photographs can show a mark; they cannot establish the condition of every joint or the right repair method.

If the table is unstable, has a broken part, or presents another safety concern, stop using it and keep it out of use while you seek appropriate advice. Do not load it, climb on it, or deliberately rock it to make a demonstration video. Ask the manufacturer or a qualified furniture repair professional how to proceed and whether transport needs special handling.

Check identification before requesting work. Use the purchase record or safely accessible label to record the brand and model. In the U.S., search CPSC’s recalls and product safety warnings, then compare the identifiers in any relevant notice with the actual item. Follow that notice’s instructions and remedy; ordinary repair shopping must not replace a recall response. An uncertain match needs clarification, and no search result is a safety certification.

For a landlord-owned piece, contact management before commissioning work or replacing it. This guide assumes you are making a decision about furniture you own.

Define one outcome before requesting an estimate

A useful request describes the result you want without prescribing a technique. For example:

I have a small side table with scratches and worn finish on the top. I want to understand whether a limited surface repair is appropriate. Please describe the proposed scope, any inspection needed, the expected appearance, exclusions, transport arrangements, and when the table could return to normal use.

Adjust the description to the actual condition. Include a whole-item photo, a closer view locating the concern, and a detail photo, provided you can take them without moving or handling the item unsafely. State any known previous treatment. If the material or finish is unknown, say so; do not infer solid wood or a particular coating from a product photo.

Ask whether the first response is a preliminary estimate based on photos or a price confirmed after inspection. Record any inspection fee and whether it is credited toward authorized work. Before sending identifying records, remove unrelated personal details and use the business’s official contact route.

Keep the model, instructions, and correspondence together. Our home product manual folder guide provides a simple product record you can reuse.

Check existing coverage before paying for work

Find the receipt, written warranty, and seller’s support information if you have them. FTC warranty guidance recommends keeping the warranty and receipt and checking what the coverage and claims process actually include, including possible labor or shipping costs.

Ask the appropriate seller or manufacturer about your particular issue before assuming it is covered or excluded. Save the reply and the next step. This article does not determine warranty rights, coverage, or what a company must provide. A missing receipt or an old purchase date is something to disclose and investigate, not enough information for this guide to decide the outcome.

Keep a possible covered remedy separate from an independent paid estimate until you know what each requires. Do not count an unconfirmed refund or credit as money already available for a replacement.

Make the two offers describe comparable work

For repair, ask what areas will be treated, what appearance differences may remain, and what is excluded. A top-only treatment and an entire-piece refinish are different jobs. If the provider cannot describe the likely result before inspecting the table, keep that uncertainty visible rather than treating it as a promise of an invisible repair.

Also ask about pickup, return delivery, the earliest available appointment, and the anticipated time without the table. If a finish or adhesive will be used, ask the provider for the actual return-to-use and care instructions; collection day is not automatically unrestricted-use day. Do not substitute a general drying time from another product.

For replacement, choose a specific available candidate that performs the same job. Check its assembled size, usable surface, assembly requirements, care instructions, and any required safety installation. Compare the whole delivery route before counting a larger table as an equivalent choice. If buying used, verify identity, condition, and recall information rather than assuming age makes a piece safer or easier to repair.

A replacement can be a different color or style, but it still needs to work beside your actual chair. If the old table is simply crowded by unrelated items, first try the useful-surface reset in our reversible living room guide.

Copy this comparison worksheet

Use actual written offers and current checkout information. Enter unknown when a detail has not been supplied; use zero only when there is genuinely no charge. Keep the date and source beside each offer so an expired estimate does not look current.

Decision item Repair option Replacement option
Result Exact area treated; expected appearance; exclusions Exact model, finish, and function in your room
Assessment Inspection cost and any credit toward work Any inspection or condition questions for a used item
Main charge Confirmed service total and what it includes Current price for the specific candidate
Transport Pickup and return, or your workable transport plan Delivery or collection; access restrictions
Other charges Tax, parts, or other items not already included Tax, assembly, required accessories, or other excluded items
Old-item plan Where it stays before and after work Keep, accepted donation, or confirmed disposal route
Timing Appointment, time away, return-to-use conditions Availability, delivery, assembly, and usable date
Uncertainty What could change after inspection Condition, availability, returns, and unresolved fit
Evidence Written scope, date, and provider contact Listing, checkout details, date, and seller contact

Add each confirmed charge once. An inspection fee credited to the final bill should not also be counted as an extra fee. Likewise, record a deposit as part of the documented payment arrangement rather than automatically adding it on top of the full quoted total. Ask the provider to clarify any ambiguous line.

Keep your own effort and time in separate notes instead of assigning a made-up hourly rate. Two collection trips, a day waiting for delivery, or nowhere to keep a second table can matter even when they do not appear on an invoice. Do not use a universal “repair if it costs less than half of new” rule: the offers may cover different outcomes, and neither ratio tells you whether the item suits your room.

Example: two repair scopes and a tempting replacement

Imagine a household whose side table has visible surface wear. In this hypothetical example, a repair professional has inspected it and identified the proposed work as cosmetic. That assumption belongs to the example; readers cannot draw the same conclusion from our pictures.

The household receives these responses:

Option What is established What still prevents a decision
Limited top treatment Addresses the worn area; some finish variation may remain Pickup and return are not included yet
Full refinishing Covers the whole piece and includes transport More work than the household originally requested; return-to-use instructions still needed
Replacement table An available listing shows a surface they like Assembled size and delivery charge have not been checked

There is no honest cheapest option yet. The household requests the missing transport charge for the limited treatment, asks about its expected appearance, and checks the replacement’s size. They compare complete totals only after those answers arrive.

Suppose they then decide the current wear is acceptable and the inspected table still meets their needs. Waiting is a reasonable cosmetic choice: they keep the record and set a date to reconsider the appearance, without purchasing a substitute. If safety or function were unresolved, waiting would mean keeping the item out of use while arranging the next assessment, not continuing to use it because the budget is tight.

Decide what happens to the old piece

EPA’s waste guidance includes considering repair for worn furniture and checking what local recycling programs accept. It supports considering repair as an option; it does not prove that every repair is cheaper or establish a carbon saving for your particular table.

If replacing, confirm the old piece’s destination before delivery day. Ask a donation organization whether it accepts the actual item and condition; do not leave it at a collection point without agreement. A recall or safety concern needs the appropriate official instructions, not a giveaway listing. For disposal or recycling, check the local provider’s accepted materials, booking process, and fees. Do not assume a mixed-material table belongs in curbside recycling.

Finish the worksheet with one decision and one next action: request a complete repair scope, choose a verified replacement, or pause an optional cosmetic change. Keep the reason with the supporting details. That makes the decision easier to revisit when the next quote arrives, without starting the comparison all over again.

Sources and further reading

  • FTC Consumer Advice: Warranties

    Keep the warranty and receipt; check coverage, service contacts, labor, and shipping terms rather than assuming a repair is covered.

  • CPSC: Recalls & Product Safety Warnings

    Match product identifiers and follow the specific official notice and remedy. A no-match search does not establish safety.

  • EPA: What You Can Do About Climate Change — Waste

    Consider repair for worn furniture, buy only what is needed, and check local recycling acceptance. No per-project environmental savings are claimed.
