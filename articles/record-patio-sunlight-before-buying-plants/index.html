Record Patio Sunlight Before Buying Container Plants

Compare two possible pot locations with a dated sunlight log, track gaps honestly, and match your patio's actual exposure to plant-specific guidance.

By Little Home Ideas · Published October 27, 2026

The sunny patch beside your patio door may disappear before lunch. The corner that stays bright all afternoon may sit in a building’s shadow the entire time. Before buying container plants, compare the actual places where their leaves will grow.

Start with two possible pot locations and a dated record. You are trying to answer a shopping question: “Which plants could suit the light I can offer?” You are not trying to turn a few photographs into a precise light measurement.

Choose two places you can actually use

Sketch the patio’s door, seating, fence, and any overhead cover. Mark Location A and Location B with fixed references, such as “beside the fence post” and “near the house, outside the door swing.” Add approximate distances in feet or inches if that helps you find the same spot again.

Choose positions that leave the door and walking route usable. Include enough room to inspect and water a mature plant, rather than fitting only the nursery pot. For help with furniture and circulation, use our small patio layout guide before reserving the remaining corners for plants.

Record the intended container and approximate foliage height. Sun on the paving does not tell you everything about the light higher up behind an opaque railing or beneath a roof. You can begin without buying pots: a sketch and photographs of the proposed positions are enough.

Rutgers Cooperative Extension’s container guide recommends choosing the location and then matching plants to its conditions. That also helps you resist buying a sun-loving plant first and trying to rearrange the entire patio around it.

Separate direct sun, filtered sun, and shade

At each location, record whether sunlight reaches the intended foliage position directly. Keep filtered light through leaves separate from a continuous direct beam, and describe building shade separately from tree shade. A bright outdoor space is not automatically a direct-sun location.

Rutgers describes full sun as at least six hours of direct sun daily, which can occur in separate periods. It also distinguishes dappled light through tree branches and leaves. Use these descriptions to understand plant guidance, while keeping your own observations more specific than a single “sunny” label.

Do not add every hour between your first and last sunny photograph. A fence, tree, or neighboring building may interrupt that interval. Likewise, three sunny checkpoints do not establish three hours of sunlight.

Observe the planting area and its shadows; do not look directly at the sun. Photos taken from a repeatable position can help you compare the shadow boundary later. They are a memory aid, not a calibrated measure of growing light.

Keep a dated log with visible gaps

Pick a reasonably clear day when you can make repeated checks. Begin in the morning, return around midday and later in the afternoon, and add observations near any change between sun and shade. This is an editorial starting method, not a validated sampling schedule.

If you need an estimate of duration, closer checks around the transitions are more useful than assuming the light stayed unchanged between widely spaced visits. Repeat on another suitable day when practical. Leave missed periods marked “unknown.”

Copy this worksheet for each location:

Record What to write
Site and date Exact position, intended foliage height, date, and weather
Observation time Clock time of each check
Exposure Direct sun, filtered sun, building shade, or weather obscuring the view
Change boundary Last check before a transition and first check after it
Unknown period Any interval you did not observe
Other conditions Overhang, tree canopy, screen, wind, hot hard surfaces, watering access
Next check Missing interval or growing season that still needs observation

Cloud passing overhead and a building blocking the sun are different causes of shade. Record which you can identify; if you cannot tell, mark the cause uncertain. That keeps the log useful without making it more precise than the evidence.

Work through a two-location example

Imagine a household comparing two corners for one new container. These are hypothetical observations, not measurements from the illustrated patio.

Check Location A: near fence Location B: beneath overhang
9:00 a.m. Building shade Building shade
10:00 a.m. Direct sun Building shade
Noon Direct sun Building shade
2:00 p.m. Missed check Missed check
4:00 p.m. Building shade Direct sun
Next step Check the start and end of its sunny interval Check when afternoon sun begins and ends

A looks promising at lunchtime, but this record does not prove it has six hours of direct sun. B’s late sunny check does not establish a full afternoon either. The useful outcome is a focused follow-up, not an automatic full-sun or shade classification.

Suppose the household wants a tomato. University of Maryland Extension gives six to eight hours of direct sun for warm-season crops such as tomatoes. Neither column yet establishes that exposure. The household can finish the missing checks, investigate another location, or ask a local nursery or extension office about a different plant suited to the documented conditions. It does not need to buy the tomato to discover the gap.

Compare light with the exact plant and season

Keep a separate candidate card: plant name and variety, intended growing months, supplier’s light instructions, mature size, recommended container, and the location being considered. Ask what an ambiguous label such as “part sun” means for that plant in your local summer conditions.

Do not treat October observations as a substitute for summer observations. The seasonal sun path and changing tree cover can alter the pattern. Keep today’s log as a dated starting point and recheck during the intended growing season. In colder areas, that may mean planning now and waiting to choose plants.

Maryland Extension’s salad-table guidance changes exposure recommendations between cooler and hotter parts of the year. That is a useful illustration of why the crop and season matter, rather than a planting calendar to copy across the United States. Check regional timing with your local extension service.

Sunlight is only one part of the decision. Maryland also identifies watering access and heat from hard surfaces as location concerns. Note a reflective wall or hot paving without assuming that a long sun interval guarantees a suitable growing spot. A light log cannot establish irrigation needs, balcony load capacity, or wind stability.

Leave the nursery with one workable choice

Bring your location notes and unresolved questions when shopping. If a candidate’s requirements remain unclear, pause the purchase or choose a better-documented option rather than filling every empty corner at once.

Once you have a suitable location and plant, our three-pot container garden plan covers the next decisions about containers, drainage, and care access. Start with the number you can maintain; three is an example, not a requirement.

Revisit the log after adding a privacy screen, moving a tall planter, or changing an overhead cover. Those changes can alter the location you assessed. For indoor greenery, use the separate window-side houseplant light check; an outdoor patio record does not describe conditions behind glass.

Sources and further reading

  • Rutgers Cooperative Extension: Outdoor Container Gardening with Flowering and Foliage Plants

    Choose the location before matching plants; direct-sun duration and dappled light are different observations.

  • University of Maryland Extension: Growing Vegetables in Containers and Salad Tables

    Different crops have different sunlight needs; hard surfaces can increase heat stress, and access to water matters. Seasonal salad-table advice is regional, not a nationwide calendar.
