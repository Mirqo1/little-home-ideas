Small Patio: Dining or Reading? Choose One Main Use

Choose a reading-first or dining-first setup for a small patio using everyday habits, real tabletop needs, and an honest plan for switching and storage.

By Little Home Ideas · Published October 31, 2026

A small patio can hold furniture for several activities without doing any of them especially well. The dining chairs may be the wrong place for a long reading session. The lounge chair may leave no useful surface for dinner. A folding table may spend most of its time leaning in the only storage corner.

Choose which activity gets a ready-to-use setup, then decide whether the second activity deserves a workable occasional arrangement. The question is not whether a catalog shows both. It is whether your household will actually use and reset the space.

Describe an ordinary outdoor occasion

Write down what you want to do, with whom, and at what time of day. “Read alone after work with a drink” gives you a clearer starting point than “outdoor living.” “Eat dinner together at the table” is more useful than “entertaining.”

Use recent experience when you have it. If you have not used the patio yet, mark your ideas as expectations and revisit them after trying the space. A week of bad weather is not evidence that you do not like outdoor dining. Think about the part of the year and the weather in which you would realistically use the patio, rather than assuming October feels the same across the United States.

Discuss the choice with everyone who uses the space. Frequency is one consideration; another person’s access needs or the one outdoor meal you both value may change the priority. This is a household decision, not a score that automatically awards the patio to the most frequent user.

Give reading and dining different requirements

For a reading-first setup, start with the seat. Consider the position you prefer, the support you want, and whether you can get in and out comfortably. If you can try a candidate chair in a showroom, use it in the position you intend to use at home, within the store’s rules and the product’s instructions. Keep a small, reachable surface for the drink or book if you need one; an entire dining table is not automatically necessary.

For dining-first, start with the seated meal. Check the chair and table together, including armrests, legs, and the space under the top. A chair that slides under an empty table in a photograph may behave differently with someone seated and getting up. Do not assume a lounge chair and dining table work together simply because their finishes match.

Make the surface test concrete. On an existing table indoors, lay out your normal plates, glasses, and anything you actually keep on the table during a meal. Compare that arrangement with a paper outline of the candidate tabletop’s size and shape. Add serving dishes only if they belong in your usual meal; if food stays indoors, record that instead. The outline checks the tabletop job, not patio circulation or structural suitability.

A place for two mugs is a coffee setup. It may be all you want, but do not describe it as a dinner setup until it can support the meal you have in mind.

Treat adjustable and folding as separate questions

Adjustability describes how an item changes position. Folding describes how it changes for storage. Neither label tells you how comfortably it supports both activities or how much effort the change requires.

For example, IKEA’s U.S. NÄMMARÖ reclining chair page describes six back positions and a folding design. That is a feature description for one model, not proof that it pairs with your dining table. Check the exact chair in the intended position and ask for any missing in-use or folded dimensions.

The U.S. TÄRNÖ table page says folding involves loosening a screw. That specific detail matters more to a daily switching routine than the word “foldable” alone. Read and follow the exact item’s instructions, including any securing mechanism, rather than using a general folding method from another product. These examples explain what to look up; neither is a tested product recommendation.

Before buying for two uses, describe the whole switch: remove the contents, move or adjust the furniture as directed, bring out the other items, and put everything away afterward. If you cannot say where each displaced piece goes, you have a storage question to resolve before a shopping decision.

Example: frequent reading, occasional dinner

Imagine a household expecting six solo reading occasions and one shared outdoor dinner during a favorable-weather week. These are hypothetical preferences, not observed readership data or a prediction of how anyone will use a patio.

Their reading-first plan has one outdoor chair and a small surface. Their dining-first plan has a table and two upright chairs. Before buying, they discover that the reading-first plan’s occasional dining arrangement depends on storing a second table inside, but they have no available place for it.

They remove that unworkable promise from the plan. One option is a reading-first patio while shared dinners stay indoors. Another is a dining-first patio with chairs they have also found acceptable for their actual reading sessions. A third is postponing the purchase until a compatible arrangement is identified. The right answer depends on what the household values; the six-to-one count does not settle it.

Suppose they agree that reading is the main purpose and dinner outside is optional. They choose to investigate the reading-first setup and keep the buying list to the chair and small surface. They do not add a folding dining set merely because it can theoretically disappear between meals. The benefit is a decision with a manageable everyday routine, not a claim that fewer pieces always look better or that this arrangement fits your patio.

Check the preferred option in the actual space

Once the activity decision is clear, use our small patio layout guide to map the actual furniture in use, doors, and walking routes. Preserve required access and each household member’s movement needs. Do not turn a sketch into a universal clearance rule.

You can also explore an arrangement in the Patio & Balcony Layout Planner. Its example objects and door markers are visual aids: the tool permits overlaps and does not check door swings, accessibility, wind, mounting, or structural capacity. Confirm actual item dimensions and the applicable requirements separately. For a balcony, resolve building restrictions and permitted use with management before selecting furnishings or containers.

Check the proposed seat at the times you intend to use it. If privacy is the obstacle, the renter-friendly patio privacy guide helps identify the particular sightline before considering a screen. Avoid adding another object until you know what problem it solves.

Include putting everything away

A reading chair’s cushion needs a real home when its care instructions require storage, just as a dining setup’s folded pieces do. Our small-home cushion storage guide starts with the actual stack and opening rather than a storage-box photograph.

Outdoor use does not mean maintenance-free or suitable for every exposure. Both linked IKEA examples provide product-specific protection and storage guidance, including indoor dry storage when possible and ventilation when covered outside. Follow the instructions for your exact furniture and cushions. A product described as outdoor furniture is not a promise that it can stay unprotected all year in your location. For seasonal decisions, use our patio winter storage plan.

Finish with one sentence: “This patio will be ready for ___; doing ___ instead will require ___.” If the last blank contains a change nobody wants to make, simplify the plan before buying. That sentence is more useful than a shopping list for two complete outdoor rooms.
